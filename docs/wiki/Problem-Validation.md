# Problem Validation

This page records the work done to confirm that the problem OriginPass addresses is real, that it affects the people named in the [Product Vision Board](Product-Vision-Board), and that existing options do not solve it.

## Problem statement

A buyer standing in front of a physical product cannot tell whether it is genuine. The producer who made it cannot prove, after the product leaves their hands, that a particular unit is theirs. The gap is worst for protected-origin crafts, where an imitation costs a fraction to produce, sells beside the original, and looks close enough at arm's length.

Three consequences follow:

1. The producer loses the sale and, when the copy is poor, the reputation attached to the craft.
2. The buyer pays a genuine-article price for something that may not be one.
3. The denomination of origin, which exists to protect a region's work, stops carrying information a buyer can act on.

## Assumptions to test

The problem statement rests on four assumptions. Each is stated so it can be shown false.

| # | Assumption | How it would be disproved |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | Producers of protected-origin crafts lose meaningful sales to imitations | Producers report imitations as a minor or absent concern |
| A2 | Buyers want to check authenticity at the point of purchase | Buyers say they do not care, or would not spend time checking |
| A3 | Existing verification options do not reach small and informal producers | Producers report an affordable option they already use |
| A4 | A check requiring an account or an app install would not be used | Buyers say they would happily install an app to verify a purchase |

### The finding that matters: legal protection exists and is barely used

Colombia protects twelve craft traditions with a denomination of origin. A denomination only has effect through an **authorisation of use**, which is what actually entitles a producer to put the protected name on what they make. The SIC publishes how many of those are registered for each one.

| Craft | Protecting resolution | Authorisations of use registered |
|---|---|---|
| Tejeduría Wayuú | 71098 of 7 December 2011 | 40 |
| Sombreros de Sandoná | 69304 of 30 November 2011 | 13 |
| Tejeduría San Jacinto | 70849 of 6 December 2011 | 6 |
| Mopa Mopa Barniz de Pasto | 70002 of 30 November 2011 | 3 |
| Cerámica Artesanal de Ráquira | 66272 of 29 November 2010 | 3 |
| Sombrero Aguadeño | 69362 of 30 November 2011 | 2 |
| **Tejeduría Zenú** (the sombrero vueltiao) | **71097 of 7 December 2011** | **1** |
| Guacamayas | 30000 of 19 June 2009 | 1 |
| Cerámica del Carmen de Viboral | 71791 of 12 December 2011 | 1 |
| Sombrero Suaza | 29488 of 1 June 2015 | not published |
| Chiva de Pitalito | 23889 of 8 May 2017 | not published |
| Cerámica Negra de La Chamba | 72993 of 11 December 2019 | not published |

Read together with the interviews still to be run, three things stand out.

**Uptake is negligible.** Across the nine denominations that publish a figure, seventy authorisations of use exist in total, and the median is three. These protections have been in force for between seven and seventeen years.

**The sombrero vueltiao has exactly one.** Tejeduría Zenú has been protected since December 2011. In the fifteen years since, one authorisation of use has been registered: a collective one held by the Resguardo Indígena Zenú de San Andrés de Sotavento, covering the delimited zone that includes Tuchín. The SIC page states the validity of that authorisation as 9 December 2025, a date now passed.

**The mechanism is not the obstacle.** Tejeduría Wayuú was protected by the adjacent resolution number, on the same day, for the same kind of craft, and has forty. So a denomination of origin can reach producers. For most of them it does not.

### What this evidence does and does not settle

It bears directly on **A3**, that existing verification options do not reach small and informal producers. The strongest option available to a Colombian artisan — a state-granted denomination of origin — reaches a median of three producers per protected craft, and one in the case this project takes as its example.

It does not settle A1, A2 or A4. Whether producers lose meaningful sales, whether buyers would check, and whether a check requiring an account or an app would be used are questions about behaviour, and no public register answers them. Those remain for the interviews and the survey.

> A number without a citation is worth less than no number. Two of the five sources above refused automated access and are recorded as not obtained rather than estimated.

## Primary research

### Producer interviews

Target: five to eight producers, mixing artisan workshops with small registered manufacturers.

Interview guide, in order. Questions are open so the answer is not led.

1. Walk me through how one of your products reaches a buyer, from the moment it is finished.
2. Have you come across products being sold as yours, or as coming from your region, that you did not make? Tell me about the last time.
3. What did that cost you, as far as you can tell?
4. What do you do today, if anything, so a buyer knows the product is genuinely yours?
5. What happens after a product leaves your workshop? Do you know where it ends up?
6. If a buyer wanted to confirm the piece was made by you, what could you offer them right now?
7. What has stopped you from using a certificate, a seal, or a registry?
8. Who else decides whether a product of yours is considered authentic?

Record for each interview: date, role, type of producer, and the answers. Do not summarise away the parts that contradict the assumptions, since those are the useful ones.

### Buyer survey

Target: thirty or more respondents who have bought craft or branded goods in the past year.

## Conclusions

*Written once the interviews and the survey are in. State plainly which assumptions survived, which did not, and what changed in the product as a result. An assumption that failed and forced a change in scope is a stronger result than four that were confirmed.*

What can be said already, from the desk research alone:

The problem is not that Colombian crafts lack legal protection. Twelve of them have it, some since 2009. The problem is that the protection stops at the producer and never reaches the object. A denomination of origin says a workshop qualifies to use a name. It says nothing about the piece in a buyer's hands, and a buyer has no way to query it at the moment of purchase.

That gap is what OriginPass is for, and the register numbers suggest something about how it has to work. A mechanism that asks a producer to register with a state body has, for most of these crafts, produced fewer than five participants in over a decade. Whatever the reason turns out to be — cost, paperwork, or distance from the institution — a product built on the same shape of demand would land the same way. This is the strongest argument yet for keeping company onboarding to one form with two verification tracks, and for the artisan track needing no registry code at all.

## Competitive review

| Option | Who it serves | Where it falls short for this problem |
|---|---|---|
| Printed certificates and hologram stickers | Producers of any size | Reproducible in bulk; nothing ties one certificate to one unit |
| Brand-owned verification apps | Large brands | Cost puts them out of reach of small producers; a buyer would need one app per brand |
| Marketplace guarantees | Online buyers | Do not apply at the informal point of sale, where the problem is worst |
| Internal traceability and ERP systems | Manufacturers with capital | Traceability ends at the factory gate and is not visible to the buyer |
| Origin seals issued by certifying bodies | Certified producers | Prove the producer qualifies, not that the unit in your hand came from them |
